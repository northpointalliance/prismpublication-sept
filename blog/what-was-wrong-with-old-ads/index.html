What Was Wrong With the Old Way of Running Ads

By Daniel Rosenthal. Published September 18, 2026. Last updated September 18, 2026.

What is the direct answer?

Average click-through on that model has fallen from over 5% in the earliest banner ads to roughly 0.05% to 0.1% today.

What are the key takeaways?

  • Cookie and browsing-history targeting guesses who someone is, not what they want right now.
  • Google Ad Manager, Facebook Ads, and X Ads are all interruption-model stacks, a slot gets filled whether or not the ad fits the moment.

Most of digital advertising still runs on a stack built for a web that looked very different than it does now. It is worth being specific about what that stack actually does, since "old way of running ads" covers three of the biggest platforms in advertising and they all share the same core mechanic.

What did the old advertising stack actually do?

Google Ad Manager, Facebook Ads, and X Ads all target the same way: build a profile of who someone is from cookies, tracking pixels, and browsing history, then guess what that profile might want. The guess gets served into a fixed ad slot on the page, a banner, a sidebar, an interstitial between posts, whether or not it has anything to do with what the person is actually doing in that moment.

That fixed-slot model shapes the content around it too. Pages built to carry a lot of ad inventory get written to accommodate the slots, short paragraphs, forced pagination, headlines optimized to survive next to a banner rather than to inform. The copy bends around the ad stack instead of the ad fitting the copy.

Why did this produce such low ROI?

Banner ads were not always this weak. In the earliest days of display advertising, click-through rates above 5% were common. That number has collapsed since, averaging closer to 0.05% to 0.1% today. Banner blindness is well documented: once a viewer learns to visually filter out anything that looks like an ad slot, the ad stops registering at all, no matter how relevant the targeting behind it was.

The cookie-based targeting behind the ad does not fix this. It can narrow who sees a banner, it cannot make someone look at a shape their eyes have already learned to skip.

What about mobile specifically?

Mobile made the interruption model worse, not better. A banner or interstitial sized for a desktop layout has to fight for a much smaller screen, often covering the content it is supposedly running alongside. Mobile display click-through rates run a little higher than desktop, commonly cited around 0.2% to 0.5% for standard banners, but still low enough that the format is closer to background noise than a placement anyone is engaging with on purpose.

What does a contextual ad change?

A contextual ad moves the trigger from who someone is to what they just said. There is no profile to build and no browsing history to read, the actual question or message is the input, and a labeled card only shows up when it clears a real relevance check against that specific message. Nothing about the surrounding content has to bend to make room for it, and nothing about the placement depends on tracking anyone across sessions.

What is the takeaway?

The old advertising stack was not undone by one bad decision, it was worn down by the gap between guessing at intent and reading it directly. Cookie-based targeting, fixed ad slots, and banner formats that mobile never fit well all trace back to the same root cause: the ad shows up because a slot needed filling, not because the moment called for it.
